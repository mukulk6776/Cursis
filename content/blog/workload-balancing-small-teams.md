## Why task counts lie about team workload

Counting open tickets fails small teams. Two teammates might each hold five tasks—yet one person has five quick 30-minute fixes while the other holds five multi-day architecture builds plus client meetings. Balancing work fairly requires measuring **committed hours**, not raw task counts.

## Use an 8-hour workday capacity benchmark

In Cursis **Team Pulse** (available on Pro and Business plans), daily plans are measured transparently against an **8-hour capacity bar**:

- **Under 7 hours committed:** Teammate has **Available** bandwidth to help unblock urgent tasks or pick up reviews.
- **7 to 8 hours committed:** Teammate is at **Full** capacity with a realistic, sustainable daily load.
- **Over 8 hours committed:** Teammate is **Overloaded** and likely to carry spillover into tomorrow unless priorities change.

Completed commitments remain counted toward the day's total hours so people never get overloaded just because they finished morning tasks early.

## Run a 10-minute weekly workload check

Once per week, leads and teams should follow three practical steps:

### Break projects into right-sized deliverables

Split multi-week goals into concrete deliverables scoped between 1 and 4 hours each, with explicit project links, priority tags (`Low`, `Medium`, `High`, `Urgent`), and clear deadlines.

### Limit daily commitments with My Day

Encourage each teammate to pick only **3 to 5 priority tasks** inside their **My Day** planner each morning. Capping daily commitments stops invisible over-promising and makes slipped work obvious by afternoon.

### Ask before reassigning specialist work

Capacity visibility works only when trust stays intact. Treat overload alerts and Ordis AI suggestions as conversation starters—check context with the owner first before moving deep technical or creative tasks to another teammate.
